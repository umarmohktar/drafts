A Company Stole My Design. Here's Where I Landed.

Drafted from your video “A Company Stole My Design... and Started Selling It” · ~1,325 words · drafted 2026-08-01

This whole thing started about six years ago: a company copied one of my designs and started selling it. I have the emails to prove it. And yet I'm about to tell you why I think it's actually a good idea to steal people's designs.

Let me back up. A client recently asked me to build something similar-but-different to a record console I made years ago. I hadn't thought about that piece in years — and it's the design that got copied.

I'll admit my original isn't the most unique thing in the world. That said, theirs was pretty clearly a rip-off.

Let's call them Octopus Stew

It started in October 2018, when I put out the video for that piece — a record player console whose cabinet tilts back fifteen degrees. I still had a regular 9-to-5 and was working out of my garage.

Flash forward about a year and a half, and I get an email from a company. I don't want to send any negativity their way by naming them, so let's just call them Octopus Stew.

Their email basically says: hey, we like your work — want to sell your pieces at our store? I explain I wouldn't be a good fit, since I build one-off custom pieces and don't mass produce anything, and I wish them luck.

The next day they reply: that's okay, Chris, we understand. Hope you don't mind, but I tweaked one of your record player designs a bit. Great work, by the way.

A few hours later I reply: shoot me a picture.

The trail goes cold. Honestly, I forgot about it — until somebody DMed me eight months later asking if I'd seen this piece, wondering if that's where I got the idea for my console. Basically flip-flopping it.

So I contacted Stew again and suggested maybe a licensing agreement. Their reply: there are a lot of gray areas of design, inspiration, and adaptation; they acknowledged the influence, but listed ways they'd taken the piece in new directions.

My answer, more or less: I don't want to spend time arguing or hinder your business. I'm never going to mass produce the piece, so do what you feel is right. All I ask is a note somewhere on your site saying the design was inspired by Foureyes — because at that point, that's all that really mattered to me. I didn't want somebody seeing their piece and thinking I ripped it off, when, if anything, it's the other way around.

That was the end of it. A lot of people would be upset — I was, at first. But my position got more complicated over time, and it comes down to three thoughts.

Too many people, too many ideas

The first is about parallel thought and ubiquitous design. Parallel thought is two or more people independently arriving at the same idea. Imagine it's October 2007, the first iPhone just came out, and two companies simultaneously release an iPhone docking alarm clock. Surely one copied the other, right? Not really — the iPhone leads naturally, almost obviously, to that design.

Ubiquitous design is something so simple and common that it can't really be claimed as an idea at all. My go-to furniture example is the IKEA Lack table. If you saw your neighbor building a table like that in his garage, you wouldn't think he'd stolen IKEA's design. You'd think: that's the table my dog would draw if he had opposable thumbs.

On both of these, I'm a big benefit-of-the-doubt kind of guy. If there's even a slight chance something is parallel thought or ubiquitous design rather than theft, I'll err on the side of not-theft. There are just too many people coming up with too many ideas for there not to be some overlap.

Which brings me to my second thought: the burden of awareness. (Sorry, I couldn't think of a better name.) I think it's crazy to expect designers to be aware of 100 percent of the design that came before them. When I sit down to design something, I'm trying my best to be original — but I have no way of knowing if I'm accidentally copying a piece I've simply never seen.

I'm so conscious of this that I pretty much avoid furniture content online now. You might think I spend my free time watching woodworking on YouTube, but my homepage is all Magic: The Gathering, cars, sneakers, and basketball. Pretty much in that order.

That's extreme — people don't need to avoid influence the way I do. But at a dozen new designs a year, it's a numbers game: someday I'll accidentally build something really similar to an existing piece. As long as I know I didn't copy intentionally, I can have a clear conscience.

Copying can actually be a good thing

And that brings me to my third thought: I think there's absolutely nothing wrong with copying, as long as you aren't profiting off it — or, more importantly, hindering the original creator from profiting off it.

Let's be real. My entire business is designing and building original things, then making long videos telling people how to build them. If I got upset when somebody did exactly that, I'd be an idiot. Honestly, seeing it is one of my favorite things — if you build one of my pieces, keep tagging me. I also straight up sell courses that teach, in extreme detail, exactly how to build our pieces. One of the most common questions we get: can I sell the piece I build from your plans? My answer is always that I don't know that I could legally stop you if I wanted to — I just don't think it's a good long-term business model.

Copying is also one of the best ways — maybe the best way — to learn anything. If you're a beginner, you should 100 percent copy stuff. As you get more comfortable, you'll start to have your own ideas, and you will find your own voice. I believe that.

A couple of years ago I wanted to get back into drawing, and what I liked most was industrial design drawings of shoes. So I hired a guy who designs sneakers for a living to draw me a Jordan I could study and use as a baseline. It worked. I'm not as good as he is, but my drawings got way better than they would have otherwise, and the skills translated into something more original — like the drawings of my own furniture in my videos.

Bottom line: copying is good.

Where I landed

Like I said, I hadn't thought about Octopus Stew in years. But with the old console back on my mind, I checked their site. They still make a couple of variations of that console to this day.

And they credit the design inspiration.

So here's where I landed: I'm fine with it. I don't think it's a good thing, but I'm not losing any sleep over it either. Two reasons. First, I'd rather spend my time progressing myself than holding somebody else back. Second, they're not some huge company — they're just trying to make it, and I don't want to get in their way, even if I disagree.

But more importantly: nothing they're doing actually hurts me, unless I let it. And I won't.

If anything has come from this entire situation, it's the new piece. A client saw the original, the design got rethought from the ground up, and the finished console exists because of all of it. That's the silver lining. And no offense to the original, but I think this one's way better.

So if you build stuff and you're into collecting records: by all means, steal this design and build it for yourself. Nothing would make me happier.

Officially and for the record — that was the situation, and those are my thoughts.
