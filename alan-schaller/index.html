Amateurs Worry About Gear, Artists Worry About Light

Drafted from your video · ~1,102 words

Drafted 2026-08-01

There's a saying: amateurs worry about gear, professionals worry about money, and artists worry about light. The reality is there's a bit of all three combined.

It's 2026 and the photography industry, like a lot of other things, keeps evolving. A lot of the rules and advice handed out over the years maybe aren't relevant any more. My own background went from fine art and street shooting through to commercial work, which honestly I never intended. It just kind of happened on its own. But I've learned quite a lot along the way, and these are five of the most important lessons from that journey.

Experiment behind closed doors

A lot of photographers get into photography because they discover it online, and the instinct is to think, you know what, I should add my work to this. It's an easy thing to fall into. It's a trap, you see.

The whole point of the early stage is to experiment, get inspired by people and try out different things before finding out who it is you really are as a photographer. Then you get into the process of taking shots that link together, and suddenly you look like a real photographer. Practising, and publishing all of your attempts during that experimentation phase, should be done behind closed doors, not in front of people online.

Think about restaurants. If a place serves twelve different types of food, Indian, Chinese, all of this stuff, you get a bit disorientated by it and you stay away. You want to go to the restaurant that says: we are the best Italian restaurant in town. That's how you get customers. Publishing online works the same way. Wait until you have a sense of who you are before you join the melee.

Don't show 17 different styles

The same goes for your portfolio. Do experiment, try lots of different styles of photography. But at some point, don't put out a portfolio showing 17 different ones. I can do portraiture, I can do street photography, I can do all these different kinds of things. It's much better these days to be considered really good at doing one thing. All the greatest photographers, artists, chefs even, are known for doing something in particular.

The challenge after that is to straddle having a style, working within a genre, without becoming too pigeonholed or dependent on one type of lighting. It's worth keeping that in mind, and not getting too carried away. Shooting 25 different styles and posting all of them online is not the way forward.

Two lenses are plenty

Buying too much camera gear is mainly a problem with the men of the world, who fancy buying every single thing to make sure they've got everything for the photographic journey ahead. Really, it just ends up confusing you, and you're missing the point. Buying too much equipment before you have a real sense of what you're trying to do with a camera can be the worst thing you can do.

When I started shooting I had a 50mm lens, and I was shooting on the Underground a lot, always up against the wall. That's what made me think I needed a wide angle, and it ended up being a 24mm. These days I carry a 50mm and a 24mm, and 99% of the time that's all I'm carrying. You'd be surprised what you can do if you know two lenses super well and you know they suit what it is you're trying to do.

And just because I use a 24mm doesn't mean you're going to like it. That's not a reason to buy it. The best thing to do is go out and shoot, and experiment with a limited amount of gear.

There's no such thing as bad light

Pick ten different photographers out of a hat and all ten will have a different bit of advice when it comes to lighting. My personal opinion is that there is no such thing as bad light.

You'll hear rules like: you can only get contrast with sunlight, harsh light isn't good, you should only shoot in golden hour. I mention these because there are some photographers who are very dependent on certain types of lighting. It has to be sunny, or whatever it is. But you can actually get a lot of contrast, and a lot of things that might surprise you, out of a rainy day. Trust me.

As a London photographer, I can't rely on a beautiful day. I've had to learn how to get my look out of different environments and different weather scenarios. So when I turn up on a project, I'm not dependent on this or that. I can just guarantee them: yes, I can get something in my look. And that is a wonderful thing to be able to say.

Exposure rules. Are there any?

I don't really think so. Come up with the creative yourself, then expose the photograph as is necessary for the light situation and the end result you want to have. If you don't know what end result you want, that's what you should be focusing on. Think: what can I get out of this scene? Not: photographer X told me you should never crush the blacks, or you should never overexpose.

When I was printing my book Metropolis, someone we were working with told me my white tones were too blown out. That was the quote. It was too white. Why have white? The same person said that having pure black was all right. So I asked: if pure black tones are okay, why is pure white a mistake? She didn't have an answer to that one, actually, and we agreed in the end to print it the way that I wanted.

You get people who set these kinds of rules around things. You shouldn't clip the whites. You should have a wide range of tones. All this stuff. Ultimately, I've seen amazing pictures with lots of negative space, high contrast and crushed tones. I've seen amazing pictures with very gentle palettes, and colour pictures that never get anywhere near high contrast. It's about executing a vision, having an idea, being creative there, and then applying techniques like exposure, and how to use and set the camera, to help achieve that look.

Understand the rules. It's always good to understand some of the basics. But when it comes down to it, thinking for yourself, applying a dash of creativity and using what needs to be done for the shot is the real secret.
