Where Self-Taught Editors Usually Get It Wrong

An experienced editor can tell whether you know what you're doing within 30 seconds of watching your work. Twenty-five, thirty seconds into a film or a scene, and it's obvious: yeah, this person needs help.

If you're a filmmaker who edits your own stuff and you started in the last five to ten years, there's a pretty good chance you taught yourself. YouTube University has gotten so good that most of what you need is out there for free; you can become a highly capable freelance editor without ever touching the traditional industry. But some things the old-school production system teaches are very hard to learn alone, and they're often what separates a good edit from a great one. That's the stuff you learn on a bigger team, with a director and a producer ripping apart your edits and demanding more emotional stakes. Not always fun, but it makes you a better editor, because in this industry you create value by getting very, very good at telling stories. Most self-taught editors never get that experience, and it leads to problems you don't even know exist yet.

Since starting my YouTube channel I get asked for feedback on lots of films, usually short docs from early to mid-career filmmakers, and I see the same thing: gorgeous B-roll, a nice interview frame, technically impressive editing, and a film that's flat in emotional stakes and story progression. People see so much high-quality imagery now that pretty doesn't really mean anything anymore, and as gear gets cheaper, that's only getting more true.

Knowing that and knowing why are different things, and knowing what to do about it is harder still. So I called up Paddy Bird, an editor who's spent decades cutting for the biggest outlets in the world: thousands of scenes for the BBC, Nat Geo, and a ton of others. He's also the first person who taught me to edit properly, through his course Inside The Edit almost 12 years ago; I'm still leaning on that foundation. (Not sponsored, just FYI.) I wanted to know where self-taught editors like me usually get it wrong, and how to fix it.

Telling too much

That 30-second tell? In Paddy's opinion it boils down to one thing: telling too much.

If I have a bunch of facts and I just blurt them out, that's information, about as uncinematic a premise as you can get. That's news editing. What hooks people is the drip feed: tell them one thing, then another, and the brain sees the second thing through the first. Mystery is about withholding information and deciding when it will have its most impact. People don't want full disclosure. They want to be teased.

I'd take it a step further. A lot of people learning to shoot, who edit because they have to, don't even know what a scene is. The most common short film I get sent is a pile of A-roll about a topic with semi-related B-roll stitched over top. No scenes. Just interview explaining the story, with whatever shots match what's being said.

Paddy thinks that comes from never having gone through broadcast. Broadcast is relentless: a director, a series producer, an exec producer, a commissioning editor, all constantly asking: what's going on here? What's at stake? Without that pressure you get what he calls primary connections. Tell me, then show me. "I love water," cut to a B-roll shot of drinking water. Cinematic documentary editing is different. It doesn't tell you; it drops breadcrumbs and you follow. It doesn't push the audience; the audience pulls themselves towards it.

That's what separates a story from a profile. If new editors can wrap their heads around revealing information through scenes, rather than telling the audience what to think, that's a huge first step.

The best question in editing

The first thing Paddy gets his students to ask is what he calls the best question in editing: what's this scene about? It sounds deceptively simple, but it holds the key to an enormous number of decisions. If you know a scene is reflective, someone recalling something difficult from their past, you've already started answering questions of structure and shot selection. Slower shots, maybe. More symmetrical framing. You'll probably know the pacing and the tone.

His starting exercise: summarize the film like an elevator pitch. Look at the little sentence under each Netflix title. A great white shark attacks a beach town: that's Jaws. Editing is usually about eliminating what isn't necessary, and many newer editors get this backwards, adding more information and detail when the idea needs distilling down to its essentials.

Read psychology, not editing books

When I asked Paddy about resources on story structure, expecting Joseph Campbell and the usual suspects, he told me he hasn't read many editing books. He thinks one of the best ways to learn editing is to study psychology. As an editor you're studying behavior over time, and sometimes the most interesting bit in a scene isn't the answer to your question. It's the contributor staring off into the distance, or doing something funny with their face that conflicts with what they just said.

It makes a ton of sense to me: editing isn't primarily a technical skill; it's an emotional communication skill.

One of Paddy's old exercises: sit in a coffee shop with noise-canceling headphones on and just watch people. The relationships, the power dynamics. They just had an argument; she's in charge. People-watching teaches you to craft behavior over time, and that's what a lot of scenes are.

The books he does recommend are screenwriting books. If filmmaking is a family, editors and screenwriters are siblings. Twenty-five years ago he did a course with the legendary Syd Field, read Screenplay, read Robert McKee, because a screenwriter is doing the same job: editing on a page with a typewriter.

I'm a big fan of this advice, because YouTube can become an echo chamber. One video teaching something gets views, everyone copies it, and soon everyone agrees a camera without open gate is a huge problem, or whatever. Looking outside that world, to books and other disciplines, is spot on.

Know your footage

One of the best things about YouTube is speed: fast answers, faster edits. But Paddy made a compelling case for slowing down.

His process is the one he gave me back in 2014, and I've used it ever since. Watch everything down. Cut things up into bins, scene by scene. Break down your interviews properly; your rushes will tell you a lot about your character. Do they stutter? Do they have a sense of humor? Use in and out points so you've seen everything and chopped away the garbage, until the mountain of footage is fragmented into something manageable. A mountain is daunting if you look up; one step at a time, it's far less so.

I experiment with AI and tech to skip that process, because YouTube deadlines demand it. But for anything deep and meaningful there's no shortcut. As Paddy put it, your film will never be as good if you don't do the work, because you don't know what you've got. It's like writing a book review when you haven't read the book.

Then he said the thing that stuck with me most. A lot of the best editing you do is not at the timeline. It's when you're sitting on a park bench having your sandwich. Your film is cut in your head; you just press the buttons to make it materialize on the timeline.

That's totally been true in my experience. My best ideas have come while walking around, not sitting in front of my laptop binge-watching tutorials. So maybe the best advice of all is just to shut your laptop and do something else.
