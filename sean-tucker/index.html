Why We Judge our Photographs Too Soon

Drafted from your video “The Secret to Choosing your Best Photos” · ~1,320 words · drafted 2026-08-01

I was giving a talk in Amsterdam recently about our book, The Art of Street Photography, and at the end a guy stuck his hand up and asked a pretty good question. How do you know which of your photographs are the good ones?

That might sound like a silly question, but I think all photographers struggle with it at some point. I'd say we can often be the worst judges of our own work. I know I can be. I've lost track of the number of times I've shown a friend a photograph I was about to delete, only for them to say: are you mad? That's one of your best photographs.

So why does this happen? I think one of the biggest reasons is that we judge our photographs too soon.

The worst time to look

This might sound a little backward, but just after you've finished a shoot is possibly the worst time to sit down and decide which of those images are good. Rushing home to put the SD card in and zoom to 100%, sorting keepers from the bin; I've learned that can be really counterproductive.

Almost six years ago I interviewed the landscape photographer Rachael Talibart, and her approach to reviewing her images has stuck with me ever since. When she comes home she puts the day's memory cards aside for weeks; there's a queue of them on her desk, fifteen at the time we spoke. She loves making photographs by the sea almost too much, and when she gets back she's "still tripping", in no position to assess the work. If she looks too quickly she's almost always disappointed; the photographs can be great and still never match the high she's on. So instead she reviews the card at the front of the queue, images made months before. She also believes our aesthetics develop over time; there are pictures in her portfolio she didn't find for a year or two, because at first she was looking for something she'd preconceived on the day.

I love that idea. And I think the reason it works is this: if we sit down immediately after a shoot, we're often reviewing the memories of making those images rather than the images themselves.

Reviewing memories, not photographs

We're still thinking about how difficult a shot was to make, how excited we were when we clicked the shutter. Those memories attach themselves to the photographs and cloud our judgement. We confuse the emotional effort of making an image with the emotional impact it has on someone seeing it for the first time. They are not the same things at all. The viewer wasn't there. They didn't see the rain we had to dodge. They just see the finished image.

Unless we separate our subjective experience of the day from the objective image, we end up adding weight to very ordinary pictures. As time passes the memory fades, we stop thinking "that took me thirty minutes to get", and we start asking a much more valuable question: does this image stand on its own merits?

We've all clicked the shutter and thought, that was the one. We get home, pull that frame up on the screen, and it just isn't what we thought it was. We're often wrong. Worse, hyper-fixating on the one image we remember being great can make us miss the potential in everything we made around it.

What my archive taught me

I was reminded of all this recently because, and this is a little confession, until this year my storage and organisation was a nightmare. Every year I bought a new hard drive and copied across every image I'd ever made, leaving a stack of drives on a shelf and no real idea where anything was. It meant I never went back to old work.

This year I finally got organised: a proper RAID setup, every image on one drive system, my entire Lightroom catalogue rebuilt. Going back through years of work was fascinating. I found so many photographs I'd completely forgotten making, and some of them were really good. And I could see the pattern I've been describing: days where I'd hyper-fixated on one image, rushed to edit and share it, and missed loads of other shots around it that were just as good.

Partly my experience on those days had clouded my judgement. But our taste also evolves. A lot of the time we're making images instinctually, before we even understand them; come back with fresh eyes and a matured taste and we see what we were trying to do much more clearly. Older work is easier to judge honestly. So many of my favourite images are ones I initially overlooked, and plenty I loved at the time turned out pretty ordinary. Time has an incredible way of clarifying things.

So here's something I'd encourage you to try. Open up a folder of images you made a few years back, without any expectations, and just start looking through. I suspect you'll barely remember taking half of those photographs. But somewhere along the way an image or two will stop you in your tracks, and you'll go: how did I miss that?

Editing means selecting

To me, that's what editing really is. Today we use the word for pushing sliders around in Lightroom, but traditionally in photography, editing meant selecting. It was photographers sitting over contact sheets with a grease pencil, marking out the images they felt had something enduring about them. Learning to make those selections is one of the greatest skills a photographer can teach themselves, and it improves greatly with a little emotional distance.

So here's the practical version. Next time you make a bunch of images, especially for your own personal work: come home, back them up as you normally would, then leave them alone. Not overnight. Give yourself at least a week. For a longer-term project, push the boat out and make it a month; if you're feeling brave, wait three. If you're worried you won't like any of them by then, that's worth challenging: maybe we add value from our memory of the day rather than because the images are strong on their own terms. Or maybe you'll discover images that were quietly waiting for you, ones you'd have overlooked because you were too close to them. In my experience, the decisions you make after creating some distance are the decisions that stick. Sometimes the best photographs don't announce themselves immediately. They reveal themselves over time.

I've come to think of photography as happening in two stages. The first is going out there to capture: full of instinct and curiosity and emotion, that little buzz every time we click the shutter. It's the reason a lot of us fell in love with photography in the first place. The second is selecting, from everything we've made, the images with lasting value, the ones we want to share with the world. That stage requires patience. It requires time and space, because distance helps us get really honest about what we're looking at.

We spend years on the first stage, buying cameras and learning to use them, teaching ourselves to recognise good light and compose a decent frame. But a meaningful body of work relies just as much on the second. And this is true of life as well: creating distance from the things which matter to us has a remarkable way of improving our perspective. It softens the emotion of the moment and strips away the noise until only what's important remains. It improves conversations and disagreements, decisions and grief, almost everything.

That's why I think patience is such an underrated creative skill. Sometimes it's not about teaching ourselves to go out and make better photographs with our camera. Sometimes it's about giving ourselves the time and the space to recognise the good photographs we've already made.
