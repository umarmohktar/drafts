Why Cybersecurity Careers Aren't Slowing Down

My prediction is that cybersecurity is going to continue to grow. It's getting more difficult, more complex, and more of an issue for companies — and ironically, I think a lot of that has been because of AI. Even with the recent career turbulence, I suspect things are only going to get harder for companies and enterprises, and I think that translates into more career opportunities, not fewer.

Security has become a foundational layer

Years ago, security was mostly approached through protecting the perimeter — the corporate network and the computers attached to it. Now we have a lot more to think about: cloud infrastructure running throughout entire companies, APIs, mobile applications, IoT devices, identity and access management, bring-your-own-device. And then AI on top of it all — vibe-coded applications, for example.

Each year, as the technology and tools progress, things become a lot more complex, and cybersecurity has really become this foundational layer in how the modern world works. With AI acting as an accelerator, we're going to see a lot more gaps — a large majority of companies already have them in their network approaches and architecture designs. Those gaps are where the work is.

A breach is the cost of a failure

Those gaps have led to many companies disclosing breaches — some small enough to get by without ever making a PR announcement — and most of us as consumers have gotten desensitized to it. But suffering a data breach is a direct result, a cost, of a failure. Some of those failures may be things the company can't control. There is still a lot of reputational risk attached.

Just take the most recent example: Instructure, which runs the Canvas LMS. A terrible way to respond to an attack throughout the last year, and I think it's going to imprint a large reputational hit and most likely a significant customer loss over the next year. We're going to keep seeing that for big enterprises and small ones alike. The blast radius keeps growing — supply chain attacks in 2026, companies being intruded multiple times. And AI is not making any of this better. It's adding an entirely new attack surface, and a lot more complexity than a lot of companies are willing to admit.

AI has empowered the script kiddie

One of the biggest misunderstandings of attackers is that they operate in this static workflow — that their methods don't change, that they target one company at a time. They don't. They're using automation, AI-assisted voice phishing, malware-as-a-service ecosystems. The barrier to entry for attackers has been coming down for years, but AI models have now cut it down significantly, and that is going to make offense scale much faster.

Guess what happens then? Defense must scale. Right now a lot of defenders are underwater, and AI just keeps adding more noise. It's going to keep being an accelerator for adopting new strategies in your defense architecture — more investment in tools, in detection and response systems, or just more engineers. More career opportunities. Unfortunately, the company often realizes this only when it has in fact been breached.

The honest part about the job market

I tend to root myself in realism, so let me be straight about the job market. Looking at security over the last couple of years, you could say there aren't nearly as many jobs as have been advertised — and I do think that's true. There's a skill shortage gap, and while I understand why individuals and universities use that sentiment, I don't really know if there are millions of job postings today, or if those predictions were ever realized. The career pipeline is fragmented, it's more nuanced than advertised, and it puts up a lot of barriers to entry.

But for as much as I harp on the realism of this industry, I will say this: it is expanding faster than the educational pipelines can support, so we're going to see new ways of training companies and individuals in the necessary skills. It's a matter of companies realizing they need to make that investment into their security strategy. And while AI is consolidating parts of our workflows, it's also opening up new areas to consider — just look at the AI attack surface itself, one of the many new attack vectors companies now have to think about with their whole strategy.

So although I do tend toward realism and, I'll admit, negativity, I'm bullish on jobs. Security jobs are still going to be a need, and I think companies will come to that realization — often the hard way.

If you're a student, an individual interested in security, or you're in the infrastructure industry: what are you doing in your career to advance your skill sets, network with individuals, and contribute to conferences and communities? Those are the opportunities ripe for a new career or position. Generic advice as it is, I do think that is how you position yourself to ride this wave of AI and the growing security implications that come along with it.

The security industry is changing. It always has been, and it always will be. For the foreseeable future, I think that change points one way: more growth, more areas of security investment, and more opportunities for the people positioned to take them.
